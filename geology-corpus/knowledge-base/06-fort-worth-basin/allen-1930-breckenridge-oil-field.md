# THE BRECKENRIDGE OIL FIELD

# Location

The Breckenridge Oil Field includes an area of about thirty square miles, surrounding the town of Breckenridge, which is the county seat of Stephens County. It is situated one hundred miles west of Fort Worth and about the same distance south of Wichita Falls. It occupies a central position within the plains of north-central Texas.

Geologically, this field is in the Pennsylvanian area. The Cisco group of the upper Pennsylvanian period forms the surface formations, and dips beneath the Permian on the west. This immediate area takes the form of an inlier as it is surrounded by the Cretaceous of the east and south and the Permian on the north and west. It is situated near the crest of the Bend arch or "flexure," the major buried structural feature in north Texas.

The sandstones and shales underlie the limestone caps, and form the sides of the east-facing escarpments. The scarps extend in a northeast-southwest direction. The long, gently westward dipping slopes are covered with a heavy growth of mesquites and a short black-jack oak growth. Hackberry, pecan, elm and some cottonwood groves occur along the margins of the watercourses. On the rocky hillsides cat-claw bushes and several varieties of wild plum grow.

Hubbard Creek and its tributaries, Sandy, Gonzales, Pecan Branch, and Dry Branch drain the western two-thirds of the county. This drainage system extends from the southern boundary of the county to the Clear Fork of the Brazos, which traverses the northern portion of the county from west to east.

## History of Development

Breckenridge was founded in 1876, and
for more than forty years was a community of not
more than 700 people. It was thirty miles from a
railroad. It continued its existence as a cow-
man's and small farmer's town until the discovery
of oil.

By chance, 1910, a water drilling out-
fit struck a gas pocket on Lookout Mountain, near
where the Curry Pool was later discovered.

As early as 1911, an organization known
as the California Company drilled two wells about
eight miles northwest of Breckenridge. The wells
were situated on the J.W. Crudgington and W.H.Green
farms. The J.W.Crudgington well required two years
to be drilled 2,400 feet, while the Green test took
but one year. Both were failures, although indica-
tions of oil appeared in the Green test at 1,700
feet; and several of the large companies became in-

5

terested. In 1914, a test was made on the Moon Ranch six miles east of Breckenridge and was a failure.

The first assurance of commercial production in Stephens County was manifested when the W.L. Carey well near Caddo came in during May, 1916 at a depth of 2,740 feet. Although a small producer, it created much interest and enthusiasm. Other early and notable wells of this area were the L.W.Wright, the E.O.Lee, and the Manning. The last one named came in with an initial production of 2,500 barrels per day, being the first big well in Stephens County. The most spectacular well of the Caddo was the Texas Company's Sandidge No. 1, which made 15,000 barrels a day. It proved difficult to control; approximately twenty dams were built across the creek upon which it was located for temporary storage.

As early as 1911 the Producers Oil Company thousand leased fifty/acres south and southeast of Breckenridge at ten cents per acre for a period of ten years. In 1914 the Gulf Production Company leased several thousand acres, paying twenty-five cents per acre annually.

It was not until October 30, 1916, that

6

the area in the immediate vicinity of Breckenridge proved worthy of serious investigation. At this time the Production Oil Company brought in the Parks No. 1 six and one-half miles south of Breckenridge and just north of Parks Camp. Oil was found in paying quantities at a depth of about 3,200 feet with a production of seventy-five barrels. This location was thirty miles from a railroad, and no pipe lines were available. Very little interest was created by this discovery well.

On October 25, 1917, the J.S.McClesky well of the Texas and Pacific Coal Company, near the town of Ranger was drilled in with an initial production of 1,200 barrels. This new area attracted the major part of the operations in north Texas for two years. In 1919, an aggressive campaign was begun in Breckenridge area. Railroads were constructed to Breckenridge. It became a supply center and its population leaped from 1,500 to 30,000 people by January 1, 1920.

From the 1916-1921 issues of the Oil and Gas Journal the following items on the early development and progress of the field have been taken:-

7

Nov. 2, 1916,--Considerable excitement has been caused among the wild catters by the bringing in of a well on the Parks ranch, about six and one-half miles southeast of Breckenridge, in Stephens County, and leases are being taken on acreage surrounding the well and in some instances good bonuses are being paid in addition to an agreement for prompt drilling. One hundred acres about two miles from the well have brought $12.50 per acre premium. This well has been drilling for more than a year and from the 1,300 foot level down, oil and gas sands have been encountered very frequently and in some instances the operators thought they had found both oil and gas. At 2,200 feet they found oil, but decided to go deeper. At 2,300 feet a gas sand was found, and the tools were blown out of the hole. This was cased off, and the well drilled to 3,170 feet, which was 50 feet into an oil sand. The well is considered good for 75 barrels.

New locations have already been made by the Producers Oil Company, one of them being 2,000 feet north and one-half mile west of the discovery well and another is on the Smith farm three quarters of a mile southeast of the well. Other parties are expected to drill in the neighborhood, among them being the Gulf Production Company, which has secured five or six hundred acres. Further north near Caddo Sheppard No.3 is drilling in a gas sand at 1,400 feet. Wright No.1 is shut down at 435 feet and Coody No.1 is shut down at 425 feet and Soldan Bros. No.1 is drilling near Crystal Falls, the last three wells being the property of the Texas and Pacific Coal Company.

November 9, 1916:- The competition for leases in the vicinity of the Parks land, where the Producers Oil Company drilled in the discovery well a couple of weeks ago, is very keen and prices ranging from $5.00 to $50.00 an acre are being paid for mineral rights at distances ranging from one mile to nine miles from the well.

8

September 27, 1918:- That Stephens County and Breckenridge district will be one of the big pools of this oil field is fully attested by the reason that nearly every producing well increases its flow from the day it comes in. Developments are going on in all sections of the county.

October 10, 1919:- Nearly eight months have elapsed since the first scouts came into this county seeking leases on land adjoining the Pool of Eastland County. Thousands of acres were obtained before any developments had been started. One or two holes had been drilled, and the results had caused the geologists and others interested to look askance at the fields of Stephens County. And then one day seven months ago, came the news of the first big well in Stephens County. The Carey Well of the Mid-Kansas had been drilled to a depth of more than 3,000 feet, and resulted in a dry hole; not satisfied with the results shown by the drill, it was decided to shoot this well, and 500 quarts of nitroglycerine were used in the shooting. The effect of this jolt was one of the most astonishing things in the oil field. Oil was thrown for 150 feet into the air. All of the tanks which had been prepared by the company were overflowed, and this overflow filled eight land reserves with oil. The approximate flow of the gusher was 12,000 barrels. This gusher was closely followed by the bringing in of the Swensondale with a flow of 5,280 barrels, and the Knott & McCauley with 3,000 barrels each.

November 28, 1919:- Three-fourths of a mile west of Breckenridge, the Skelly Company's No.1 Branch, which was showing about 10 barrels daily was shot with 300 quarts of nitroglycerine, and is now reported to have increased its flow to about 1,200 barrels daily.

December 5, 1919:- The Texas Company is now the largest purchaser of oil in Stephens County. It has more acreage to develop than any other company operating in the county.

January 23, 1920:- Amovement is on foot at Breckenridge, county seat of Stephens County to restrict the drilling of oil wells in the city limits to one well to every four blocks. It is thought that by pooling their interests the property owners can reap the benefits of the oil underneath and at the same time keep the city free from dangerous conditions that prevailed in the other towns of the state, which had townsite drilling. It was decided at a mass meeting of the citizens in the courthouse last week to absolutely restrict the drilling of one well to every block for the present and to leave further restrictions to be decided later.

March 26, 1920:- In order to forestall the "close-up" drilling on the townsite, the city commissioners had adopted an ordinance which restricts drilling oil wells within 150 feet of property lines. The laws of the state authorized the Railroad Commission to adopt drilling rules and regulations with reference to such drilling. The citizens of Breckenridge have been warned by the commission to observe this rule when drilling in the city limits, for the two-fold purpose: first, from the standpoint of oil conservation; second, from the standpoint of reducing the fire hazard and the elimination of the unsightly view of oil derricks in the city. They have recommended that all interests in a city block be pooled and the drilling be limited to one well for a city block and have recommended that it further be restricted to one well for four blocks when the consent of the property owners can be obtained. The recommendations have been indorsed by the Chamber of Commerce.

11

August 13, 1920:- A town lot drilling epidemic has struck Breckenridge. It is reported twenty small stock syndicates are being organized for the purpose of drilling on city blocks. These companies are capitalized from about $75,000 to $100,000 and most of the stock and units have been taken by local people. At present it is estimated there are 125 wells drilling or ready to spud in on the townsite. Building activity is booming and many new sites are being selected for hotels and business houses.

August 20, 1920:- Operators in the Breckenridge pool contend that the gravity lime formation, which is found in that district does not drain like the black lime encountered in the Eastland County, Caddo, Necessity, and Gunsight fields.

September 24, 1920:- The biggest and most sensational well of the week was the Magnolia Petroleum Company's No.7 Brown, one-half mile southeast of the town of Breckenridge. Conservative reports placing the production of the well at 4,000 barrels. The pay sand was found at 3,038 feet and the lime extended for 100 feet. The flow of the oil was so great when the well was brought in that all the flow lines to the tanks were torn loose and the oil sprayed over the surrounding territory. This is one of the biggest wells in Stephens County for several weeks.

March 4, 1921:- The largest well in the North-Central Texas field was completed last Tuesday by the Walker-Perkins Company. It is the No.7 Goodwin, three-fourths of a mile northeast of the Breckenridge courthouse and is showing about 10,000 barrels initial production, all oil, at 3280 feet.

## General Characteristics of the Pay Horizon

The main pay horizon in this field is
known as the "Caddo lime," "Breckenridge lime" or

20

"False black lime;"* and occurs about 215 feet below the top of the Smithwick shale. There is some doubt as just to the exact stratigraphic position of this horizon. Some call it the top of Marble Falls limestone, but this would make the Smithwick abnormally thin in this area. It seems that this limestone is lenticular and plays out in relatively a short distance. A shale of 100 feet in thickness intervenes between this limestone and the next lower stratum, which is undoubtedly Marble Falls.

The upper 40 or 50 feet of this limestone contains oil, and is a soft porous gray limestone. The remaining 150 to 200 feet solid limestone is black and fossiliferous with a few shale breaks. The lower 20 or 30 feet also contain oil.

The Bend, including the Smithwick shale, Marble Falls limestone and Barnett shale reaches a total thickness of approximately 1200 feet in this area.

"Caddo lime," "Breckenridge," and "False black lime" are drillers' terms for the pay horizon and should not be confused with formation names.

## Origin of the Oil

The oil was undoubtedly derived from the petroliferous limestones and carbonaceous shales of the Bend group, and, under favorable conditions, it migrated into the nearby porous beds. The porosity of the sediments controlled its accumulation to a great extent, but the regional and local anticlinal structures were of greater influence.

## Relation of Production to Subsurface Structure

The subsurface structure in the Bend series of the Breckenridge field occupies a position near the axis of the Bend Arch, and is one of the first major reversals in the regional dip to the west. One of the greatest accumulations of oil in north Texas has taken place in this splendid trap. The major portion of the oil accumulation is on the west and northwest side of this structure. This position of the oil on the structure has suggested its possible source and the direction from which it has migrated.

## Relation of Surface to Subsurface Structure

The structure of the younger Pennsylvanian strata including the Strawn, Canyon, and Cisco groups, in on the whole exceedingly simple, and consists of a west and northwest dipping monocline superimposed upon the gently arched folds of the underlying Bend. There is no indication of the Bend arch and only slight evidence of the secondary folds of the Bend strata in the formations at the surface. The only deviation from the normal northwest and west dips are small plunging folds, or terraces, commonly referred to as noses, and small faulted areas. The noses are in most cases less than one mile wide and are two to three miles long. The small structures rarely occur singly; several may be associated to form small anticlinoria, or more commonly they occur in beds of slight deformation associated with small terraces and other small warpings. In several cases there will be a small fold plunging westward and a second at its side trending north or northwest with a sharp syncline between. (See diagram, figure 18). ## The Origin of the Surface Folds and Faults

The cause of the north Texas Surface folds and their relation to the larger subsurface structural features has been a subject of much interest to geologists and many hypotheses have been suggested, of which the following are accepted as the more plausible explanations:

1. That all the small noses and terraces are located over larger folds in the Bend strata and represent small post-Cisco movements along the old lines of pre-Strawn folding.
2. That the Strawn formation was laid down on a very uneven Bend surface that contained escarpments and similar to the topography to-day. That during the latter periods there has been a settling in the pre-Strawn valleys and slight lateral movements in the upper sediments. That the old Bend ridges have acted as buffers to the lateral pressures and produced a wrinkling at the surface which is responsible for the belted alignment of the small areas of deformation.

3. That there was a series of faults or fault zones produced at the end of the Bend epoch trending in northeast-southwest lines. That later slight movements in post-Cisco times these old fault planes produced slight wrinkles at the surface.

4. That the folds at the end of the Bend epoch were all connected with the uplift that produced the Llano Mountains and were caused by isostatic adjustments of the earth's crust involving the upward movement of lighter granitic masses and the settling of surrounding basic deposits. That these forces have not been active since the beginning of the Strawn epoch, and that all later movements have had no relation to the pre-Strawn upheaval but have been brought about by local causes such as adjustment to load during epochs of rapid deposition. Chemical changes during the induration of the sediments, slight contractions due to crustal shortening, etc., and that the location of the small surface over the larger folds in the Bend is purely chance. 10

## General Divisions and Nature

The Pennsylvanian formations in North-Central Texas are divided into the upper and lower Pennsylvanian. The upper Pennsylvanian is composed of the Strawn, Canyon, and Cisco groups, while the Bend group makes up the Lower Pennsylvanian. The Cisco group outcrops over most of Stephens County. These formations have a regional dip slightly north of west at a rate from 40 to 60 feet to the mile. Local surface structures interrupt this monoclinal structure. The Pennsylvanian formations form a great inlier in North-Central Texas. They dip beneath the Permian on the West and North, and the basal Comanche-rests upon them to the east and south. A thin tongue of Trinity sand extends across Eastland dividing the Pennsylvanian into major areas of exposure.

The formation will be briefly discussed in the order which they are encountered by the drill.

The Canyon group consists of four division beds of massive, hard, thick, blue limestone 10 to 100 feet thick separated by layers of gray and blue marl 250 to 300 feet thick which contain thin limestone layers and in places lentils of sandstone and conglomerate. ## Smithwick Shale

The Smithwick Shale in this field consists of about 200 feet of rather dense, carbonaceous, fissile shale with sand lentils. Below this shale there is close to 200 feet of rather solid limestone. The first 40 or 50 feet is a porous gray limestone containing oil. The limestone becomes shaley toward its lower portion, and the last 20 feet is also very petroliferous, and productive in some wells. Below this 200 feet of limestone there is 100 feet of shale which belongs to the Smithwick. This limestone is called the "Caddo lime," "Breckenridge lime," or "False black lime." It is lenticular over a broad area.

The Smithwick can be distinguished in well cuttings from the overlying Strawn shales by its darker color, greater hardness, and less tendency to disintegrate into mud.

## Marble Falls Limestone

The Marble Falls limestone is about 600 feet thick, and consists of black to gray limestone with alternating beds of black to blue shale. Sands and sandy layers are common in drill records. Pyri-

45

tized fossils are of frequent occurence. There are several possible horizons in the Marble Falls that have not as yet been developed in this area.

### Barnett Shale

This formation is approximately 100 feet thick, and rests unconformally upon the Ellenberger limestone. It consists largely of black bituminous shale with some gray to black limestone. The age of this shale body is uncertain. Some call it Mississippian in age; others place it as lower Pennsylvanian. It is remarkably uniform in thickness over a broad area.

### Cambro-Ordovician

The Ellenberger limestone formation consists of a massive white to gray, crystalline, dolomitic limestone about 1000 feet in thickness. It is charty in places. An erosional and angular unconformity separates the Ellenberger from the overlying Barnett shale. The Ellenberger is rather difficult to drill, and when it is reached by the drill, the test is usually abandoned.

# Productive Methods

# Drilling

The standard cable tool system of drilling is exclusively used in this area. The hardness of the formations prohibits the use of the rotary equipment with success. The Pennsylvanian formations are not exceedingly difficult to drill. Few cavey formations are not exceedingly difficult to drill. Few cavey formations are encountered, as long as the hole is kept relatively dry. The wells in the Breckenridge field are usually started with a 15½ inch hole. However, those that expect to drill below 3,500 feet start with a 22-inch hole. For such large bits, large tool joints are necessary to stand the vibration. In the early days many pins were "jumped" due to the use of small points.

The standard tool joints in use in north Texas on large bits is the 4x5 inch joint, seven threads per inch, T & H. This is used on bits from 20-inch to 10-inch in size.

The 8¼-inch and 6¾-inch bits use a 2¾ x 3¾ pin, 7 threads, T & H; and the 5¾-inch bits use a 2x3-inch joint, 7 threads T & H. All bits should be of the Mother Hubbard pattern... 14

It is customary to "spud in" to a considerable depth, sometimes to 300 or 400 feet. The tools are then hitched on to the temper screw and the regular drilling operation starts with the walking beam furnishing the drilling motion. The shale and limestones are drilled without difficulty, but the sands give trouble. The sharp sand cuts the bits, and they soon get out of gauge and "tight hole" results. ## Methods of Handling Water Problem

When water sands are encountered, it usually becomes necessary to run pipe or underream the last string of pipe in the hole through the water sand. Sometimes, as much as 1000 to 1500 feet of open hole is carried ahead of the pipe as long as no water sands are encountered. Water sands are usually drilled through, and casing is landed on the first limestone layer beyond. The water is usually shut off with very little difficulty. It is seldom necessary to cement or mud in the water sands in this area.

When the depth of the producing horizon is known, it is customary to land a string of casing immediately above the point where the production is expected. When this is accomplished, the entire hole above the sand is cased off. There was much difficulty in early operations by running too long strings of light weight pipe which resulted in collapsed casing.

The total recovery of all casing from an abandoned well is quite common. The casing is usually in good condition, as corrective forces are not active. In some cases pipe that was in the ground for ten years has perfect threads on extraction. In pro-

# Cost of Present Day Operations

The present cost of drilling is much less than that of the boom days. According to Otto Peters's paper presented to the Breckenridge Chamber of Commerce, "The Decline of Oil in Stephens County," the cost of bringing in a well would approximate for a 3,000 foot well about $3.50 per foot of hole and this cost would provide for the two drillers and tool dressers and the supervisors, the pipe about $8,000.00 and the rig $5,000.00 the trucking $2,000.00, the slush pit $200.00, the gas and water about $30.00 per day for about sixty day, incidentals $500.00. 16

The cost of drilling a well is variable. During the boom days with high wages and expensive material, besides the enormous prices paid for leases, it was necessary for the well to make approximately 100 barrels daily for it to be a profitable undertaking. Most leases sold for $100.00 to $1,000.00 per acre after activity became intense. One well on an 80-acre tract would probably cost in the neighborhood of $110,000, while to-day the cost would probably be less than a third of that amount. To-day, a 50-barrel well means a good profit.

## Shooting the Well

As shown, the pay horizon varies in short distances. In some places it is a hard, tight, pure limestone, while in others it is a soft, porous, sandy limestone. In the first type, it is necessary to use a shot of nitroglycerine to open up and connect the various porous spaces, and form drainage channels for the oil, so that it may collect in commercial quantities. The soft, porous, sandy limestone yields its oil readily upon being penetrated.

The use of nitroglycerine has produced remarkable results in this field. Many of the wells refused to produce until shot. Production increased from a few barrels to several hundred in several instances. It has almost become a custom to shoot a well in this field. If a large production is expected, a large shot is generally used. Wells that have to be shot usually come in for a big flush production, and fall off rapidly, while those that are relatively good without a shot of nitroglycerine maintain a more or less steady rate of production over a considerable period.

Amount of Nitroglycerine Used

In the early days, many large shots were used.

56

Some of them did much damage to the hole. Frequently a large shot would cause the Smithwick shale to cave or fracture the casing. The size of shot depends upon the expectations of the operation. Ordinarily the amount used upon the thickness of the oil bearing formation and the diameter of the hole. The nitroglycerine charge is put in shells of tinned sheet metal, and lowered to the desired depth. For general use, the shells have 3, 5, and 7-inch diameters, and hold respectively ten, twenty, and thirty quarts.

## Methods of Firing

When a well is "shot," the lowermost string of casing is pulled up at least 400 feet above the top of the water placed above the shot. If the water is allowed to extend up into the casing, the force of the shot will burst the casing at the level of the water. The height that the casing is raised depends upon the size of the shot. For shots up to 100 quarts 100 feet above the water level is sufficient, but for a shot of 500 quarts, it is customary to remove the inner string of casing entirely.

## Gasoline Plants

At present the gas is the most important product from the wells. There are 37 gasoline plants and 7 carbon black plants within a short distance of Breckenridge. The manufacture of gasoline has become one of the most important operations of the oil industry. In the summer of 1929, Stephens County plants were turning out daily 112,000 gallons of this product. The gas in this field yields about one and one-half gallons per thousand cubic feet of gas.

## Carbon Black Industry

This locality is the highest producing area of carbon black in the state of Texas, and rates about fifteen in the world. Carbon black is made from the residual gas from which gasoline has been extracted. The lean gas in Stephens County yields between one and one-and-one-tenth pounds carbon black per 1,000 cubic feet. The carbon black plants have an average daily capacity of 125,000 pounds of black. The price of this product ranges from four cents to thirty cents per pound.

## Conclusion

The Breckenridge development was a result of extensive geological work in north Texas by various companies. The Texas Company was one of the early active investigators. The exploitation of the Breckenridge field followed the developments at Ranger. The Bend structure of this field is that of a broad dome. It presents a distinctive reversal of dip near the axis of the Bend arch, and acts as a splendid reservoir for the oil. This reservoir has yielded the majority of the 117,157,138 barrels of crude produced by Stephens County up to January 1, 1930.
